Beyond the Fitting

Access, dignity and everyday participation in prosthetic care for women in India.

Team
Ishita Sarin & Moulik Nanda
Field
6 days, BMVSS Jaipur Foot, April 2026
Output
Service model · Chappal Lock · research paper (submitted to IndiaHCI 2026)
In 30 seconds

The gap

Women were missing from prosthetic care in India, and once fitted, the prosthesis still didn't fit their floor-sitting, chappal-wearing, mehendi-wearing lives.

What I did

Six days at BMVSS, 15 interviews, journey maps, a service blueprint and three printed prototypes.

What came out

A community-linked service model, the Chappal Lock, and a research paper submitted to IndiaHCI 2026.

Why I did this

I've always noticed who a system is built for, and who it quietly leaves out.

Growing up, I watched a woman with cerebral palsy struggle to get out of her van at our bus stop while people laughed or looked away. That stayed with me. When I began looking at prosthetics, I started with manufacturing: who makes them, for whom, at what cost and how far they reach.

At BMVSS the question changed. Among roughly fifty people a day, I kept counting two or three women. That number stayed with me more than any statistic I found later. It made me ask what keeps women from reaching care, and why a prosthesis that works can still not fit a woman's life. Dignity, for me, became something concrete: wearing your own chappal, sitting on the floor to cook, putting on mehendi for a wedding.

My role: Manufacturing research, 15 interviews (5 with women), synthesis, journey maps, service blueprint, CAD and 3D-printed prototypes, co-authored the paper

A moment that stayed with me
One woman had painted her prosthetic leg with nail polish.

It wasn't decoration. It was her way of making it her own, and it reshaped what dignity meant in this project.

The gap I noticed

What I saw at BMVSS matches the wider picture: a 2026 review of 14 Indian studies (7,272 patients) found 81.3% were men. Women travelled long distances, depended on family to get there, and often found the centre by chance.

Women who were fitted still couldn't do ordinary things: wear juttis, sit on the floor to cook, put on mehendi for a wedding.

How might we improve access to prosthetic care for women who face social and mobility barriers?

How might we help women adapt their prosthesis to their culture and social life?

Designed for India, not an "average" user

Jaipur Foot was built for Indian lives: low-cost, rubber, made for walking barefoot and squatting. But a device designed around one set of assumptions can still shut out others. In the field, the gaps were rooted in culture, climate and how Indian homes work.

Life on the floor

Cooking, prayer and meals happen sitting on the floor. Many removed the prosthesis before sitting down.

Chappals and juttis

Backless footwear slips off with no toes to grip, often without the wearer noticing.

Mehendi, payal, bichiya

Women wanted to wear them, and avoided mehendi on the prosthetic limb for weddings and festivals.

Skin tone

A mismatched skin tone made women self-conscious in public and social settings.

Sarees and heat

Sarees snag on exposed callipers, metal rods heat up and irritate skin, and velcro causes blisters.

Monsoon and fields

Rain changes stability, and one fixed foot can't handle farm terrain, cycling or heels.

Distance and family

Long journeys, cost, and family decisions often decide whether a woman reaches care at all.

ASHA and the panchayat

India already has trusted community health networks. My service model routes through them instead of building new ones.

What I did

  1. Mapped the landscape

    Our cohort of 15 clustered the topic into five areas. I led Manufacturing, comparing prosthetic organisations by users, cost, maintenance and reach.

  2. Six days in the field

    Observed fitting and service, spoke with founder Dr. D. R. Mehta, and ran 15 open-ended interviews myself, five with women.

  3. Synthesis

    Affinity mapping across about 95 participant accounts from the cohort, an insight tracker of about 27 insights, and two journey maps.

  4. Two directions from ten

    A decentralised service model, and a small footwear lock.

  5. Three rounds of prototypes

    Each one CAD-modelled and 3D-printed, then tested informally with the team and peers.

What I found

Available ≠ accessible

People knew the centre existed and still couldn't reach it: travel, cost, information, family decisions.

Chance

A farmer brought his daughter through Rishikesh and Chandigarh before someone mentioned BMVSS.

Ownership

One woman painted her prosthesis with nail polish. Personalisation was a way to make it hers.

A chappal slipped.A garment caught.Rain changed the stability of the prosthesis.

What I made

A service that reaches women. ASHA workers, PHCs and the panchayat identify people early. Coordinated transport, outreach camps and mobile 3D-scanning units bring care closer. After fitting, home follow-ups cover sitting, uneven ground and hygiene.

The Chappal Lock. A small clip and strap that holds a chappal, jutti or mule on a prosthetic foot, without changing either. Round 2 broke at a drilled hole under load. Round 3 evened the grooves. A flat-sided version also helped people without prosthetics.

Anyone who needs a steadier step
Older adults
All prosthetic users
Women with prosthetics

Design for all, by designing for niche

Who the Chappal Lock was for, and who it turned out to help

It started with a few women at BMVSS. Their chappals slipped off their prosthetic foot without them noticing, worse in the rain, so they stopped wearing the footwear they loved.

What I learned

Available isn't accessible

A centre can exist and still be out of reach. Access is travel, money, information and family.

Design for the edges

A lock made for a few women turned out to help older adults too.

Testing with peers isn't testing with users

My next step is putting the lock on the feet it was made for.

iAIM Hospital Diet System

Following a patient's meal from the doctor's prescription to their tiffin.

Team
Ishita Sarin & Iniya R
Where
iAIM Healthcare Centre, Bengaluru, March 2026
Output
Visual patient booklet
In 30 seconds

The gap

Diet is treatment at iAIM, but instructions reached patients as dense clinical text.

What I did

Mapped the system, followed one meal end to end, and read it through pattern language.

What came out

A visual, multilingual patient booklet the hospital chose.

Photo: I-AIM Healthcare Centre

Why I did this

In an Ayurveda hospital, food isn't hospitality. It's prescribed, like medicine.

I was curious how something so personal is coordinated at scale: a different diet for each patient, every day, passing between doctors, nurses, a diet committee, the Customer Relations Officer and an outsourced kitchen. I followed the diet system because it was where the hospital was most complex and least visible.

The meals were careful and well made. What surprised me was the gap in understanding: a patient guide written almost entirely in clinical text, for people who might not read well. Care that patients can't understand isn't complete care. That was the accessibility gap I wanted to close.

My role: Observation, ecosystem map, customer journey map with data, pattern language, ideation and booklet prototype

A moment that stayed with me
Every tiffin carried a small handwritten chit with a room number.

The whole system ran on details like that: paper, memory and people who cared enough to check.

What I did

  1. Observed the diet system

    Planning, portioning, packing, tasting by nurses and the Customer Relations Officer, and delivery to rooms.

  2. Ecosystem map

    The diet system turned out to connect patients, doctors, the CRO, the diet committee and the kitchen.

  3. Journey map with data

    The CRO's numbers on patient mix, diets and staffing grounded our work in the system's real scale.

  4. Pattern language

    Pramod Khambete's method: patterns already in the system, and ones we could add.

  5. Chose with the client

    From four ideas, the CRO picked the booklet. We dropped a diet kit because of budget and licences.

Service design tools

Instead of jumping to solutions, we used pattern language to read the journey map: what already works in the system, and what could be added. That gave us three opportunity areas: diet understanding and transparency, communication and coordination gaps, and food flow and waste visibility.

What I found

4:30 pm

Cut-off to change tomorrow's diet. Anything later goes through a WhatsApp group.

3 copies

Of every daily chart: diet committee, kitchen, patient. Tiffins carry handwritten room chits.

50%

Of patients are on special diets. About 80% are in-patients, at roughly 1 staff to 6 patients.

What I made

A visual companion that replaces a text-heavy guide: "What my body needs today", "What to expect" at each stage, and foods colour-coded as safe, careful or avoid, with the reasons. Multilingual, told as a story, and usable by patients who can't read well.

What it taught me: smooth systems run on cut-off times, duplicate copies and informal channels. That's where errors hide, and where small redesigns help most.

What I learned

Look at the handoffs

Cut-offs, copies and WhatsApp messages are where systems are most fragile.

Let the client choose

The CRO knew which idea would actually get used. That saved us from building the wrong thing.

Visual beats verbal

For mixed literacy, colour and pictures do more work than paragraphs.

Community Palliative Care

Four days with a community palliative care team in Kundapura, and the learning artefacts we made with them.

Team
Ishita Sarin, Gouri & Nikkon
Where
Vandse, Alur & Hakladi, Kundapura, Karnataka
When
February 2026
Made
System map · 4 accordion artefacts · exhibition
In 30 seconds

The gap

Care work is relational and mostly invisible, even to the people doing it.

What I did

Days on home rounds, a system map, and 12 care stories turned into 4 accordions.

What came out

Artefacts the care team corrected, and a new meaning of care for me.

Why I did this

I came to Kundapura thinking palliative care meant the last stage of life, and that care systems were made of equipment, forms and schedules. I wanted to test that by being inside a care system instead of reading about it.

Place-based learning gave us that: days alongside a team that visits people in their homes, week after week. One question kept me going: what actually holds this system together? The answer wasn't in any workflow. It was in trust, familiarity and people adjusting for each other.

The accordions were our way of making that visible, and of giving something back to the team that let us in.

My role: Field observation and documentation, system mapping, accordion artefacts, exhibition

A moment that stayed with me
The care team picked cashew apples for us and showed us how to eat them.

Care, here, wasn't only for patients. It was how the team treated everyone, including us.

What palliative care means to me now

Before this visit, I connected palliative care with the end of life. In Kundapura I saw something much wider: a relational, community-based system of care for anyone who needs continuous support. That includes people with neurological conditions, mental health conditions, age-related challenges, or anyone who can't fully care for themselves.

Care was wound dressing, blood pressure and sugar checks, and physiotherapy. It was also a counsellor helping someone relearn how to count, a team that knew a caregiver's tea preference, and extra diapers carried "just in case".

The system works not because of efficiency alone,but because of care, trust and belief.

How we learned

This was place-based learning: understanding a system by being inside it, with the people who run it, instead of studying it from outside. One of us asked questions, one took notes, and one sketched on site.

  1. Before the visit

    Secondary research on palliative care, community health and the Samagra Arogya model of care.

  2. Days 1–2: On rounds with the care team

    Home visits in Vandse, Alur and Hakladi. We observed care in practice, the team's workflow, documentation and route planning, and how ASHA workers use the Need Assessment Form to bring people into care.

  3. Day 3: Making sense of it

    Refined our system map, colour-coded types of care, mapped the layers of care infrastructure, and turned 12 care stories into 4 accordions.

  4. Day 4: Giving back

    An exhibition for the care team, the gram panchayat president, visiting students and professors.

What I understood about the system

I first saw infrastructure as equipment: tablets, vehicles, route maps, forms. In Kundapura it showed up as people and relationships.

Trust

The relationship between the care team and families is what makes visits work.

Connectors

ASHA workers link families to the team. Saturdays are for updating schedules, equipment and records.

Flexibility

When a team member is away, routes and roles shift so no one is missed.

The team: a counsellor-coordinator, two physiotherapists, two nurses, two drivers and a helper, caring for 305 people across seven gram panchayats.

Why accordions

Care doesn't happen as isolated events. It unfolds in layers, and most of the coordination happens backstage. An accordion let us show that:

  1. One layer at a time

    Each fold opens the next part of a care story, the way we came to understand it.

  2. Connections, not events

    People, visits and decisions sit side by side, so the links between them are visible.

  3. Visible and invisible care

    The front: wound dressing and visits. The back: planning, rerouting and record-keeping.

The artefact started as documentation and became a learning tool. At the exhibition, the care team recognised themselves in it, pointed out what we had missed and corrected us. We stopped collecting information and started learning together.

The accordions

Four accordions, each unfolding one care story panel by panel: who the care seeker is, what the team does in the room, what happens backstage, and what we were left wondering. Sticky notes mark what we observed, what we asked and what we still didn't know.

What we made

What changed in me

I used to collect as much information as I could and treat learning as something I gather. Now I know the most valuable insights come from watching interactions, not asking direct questions. What sustains a system often isn't written in any workflow. Artefacts can mediate between people. And systems are lived by people.

Ethics in practice: consent before every photo, identities kept intact, and knowing when to stop asking.

What I learned

Watch, don't just ask

The best insights came from interactions, not interview questions.

Artefacts are conversations

The accordion worked when the team started correcting it.

NeuroSpace

It is 2081. A neural implant records everything you live, and a company called mémoire keeps it. For some, the fear isn't forgetting. It's remembering too much.

Team
Solo
Made
2 physical artefacts · 2 software prototypes · a web world · a film

In 30 seconds

The gap

Talk about memory tech stays abstract. People read the risks but don't feel them.

What I did

Wrote a world in 2081 and nine lives inside it, then built it as objects, software, film and a website.

What came out

Odeur and Cinq Sens, Memory Book, Memory Compression, and a live web world.

The gist, in two minutesFrom cave walls to the cloud to memory sharing: a montage I edited to set the scene for 2081.

Contains third-party footage (including Kurzgesagt and BBC), used for educational purposes.

Why I did this

What happens when technology stops being a tool and starts holding parts of us?

As a Computer Science engineer who became a designer, I keep coming back to that question. Memory felt like the most personal place to ask it. We already keep our lives in phones and clouds. I pushed that one step further: if every memory is recorded, who owns it? Who can delete it, sell it or read it? And who pays the cost?

Writing about that future felt too distant, so I built it. Turning nine lives into a world I could code forced me to make every rule concrete, and lets people feel the consequences instead of reading about them.

My role: Concept, worldbuilding, writing, interaction design and code

A moment that stayed with me
For some, the fear isn't forgetting. It's remembering too much.

That one line became the emotional centre of the world.

One future, four ways in

I didn't want NeuroSpace to live only on paper. So I kept returning to the same world from different directions: as a story, as objects you could hold, as software you could use, and as a film. Each one tested the idea in a way the others couldn't.

1. The world

I wrote the rules of 2081 through nine perspectives, from the company that keeps everyone's memories to a child who isn't allowed to see her own until she turns 18. Then I built it as an interactive website: a 40-second explainer, an archive of nine lives, a briefing before each one, and a "what this shows" card at the end.

Step into NeuroSpace ↗ Best in Chrome, with headphones.

2. Objects from 2081

My first six ideas (compression, smell, prompts, face tracking, search, touch) turned out to be the same archive entered through different senses. So I built devices for retrieving a memory.

Artefact 1

Odeur

Can a scent bring back one specific memory, for one specific person?

  1. Who are you?

    Iris detection on the base unlocks the device only for its owner, because memories are personal.

  2. How strong?

    A modulator dial sets the intensity, from a hint to a strong scent.

  3. Remember

    The matching scent is released, and the device rolls toward you on wheels.

Tested Wizard-of-Oz style. Its limit was clear quickly: one sense isn't enough.

Artefact 2

Cinq Sens

What if retrieval isn't locked to one sense, but all of them?

  1. Recognises people and pets

    Iris detection for humans, a paw sensor for animals (with a laser that helps them stay still), and a thermal sensor that reads body heat.

  2. You choose the sense

    A regulator dial picks smell, taste, sound, video, or all of them.

  3. It plays the memory back

    A smell module, a taste-bud module, audio, and a small video projector.

3. Software that remembers

Then I built the same logic as two working web prototypes, using the camera, microphone and in-browser machine learning.

Prototype 1

Memory Book

Find a memory the way your mind does.

  1. Touch

    Hold an object up to the camera, and the memories tied to it surface.

  2. Search, smell or describe

    Type or speak, describe a scent, or just describe what happened.

  3. See the cost

    Each sense has a storage cost. Smell, touch and taste cost more than audio or video, which makes "too much memory" tangible.

Prototype 2

Memory Compression

You can't keep everything. What stays sharp?

  1. Upload a memory

    Any video becomes a memory to keep.

  2. Choose what matters

    Mark the parts that stay sharp. Everything else fades into colour blobs.

  3. Build what works

    I tried Meta's SAM2 for tracking, but it needed a GPU server. So I built a simpler version myself that runs in the browser.

I also rehearsed this future outside the screen: the montage at the top of this page, which traces how humans have stored memory, and a kit-bashed physical artefact.

What I learned

Imperfect first is fine

Odeur only used smell. That showed me it needed more senses, so Cinq Sens came next.

Build what you can run

When SAM2 needed a GPU and money, I built something simpler myself, and it worked.

Medicare

Medication reminders for older adults that don't depend on a phone.

In 30 seconds

The gap

Missed doses and mixed-up medicines, and reminders people don't want to carry.

What I did

Mapped pain points and sketched three ways to remind without a phone.

What came out

A necklace, a watch band and MediClock+, sharing one language: vibration, light, colour.

Why I did this

Missing a dose sounds small. For older adults and people managing chronic illness, it adds up: medicines that look alike, routines that change, and reminders that live on a phone they may not use.

Many existing reminders felt clinical, hard to use, not portable, or like something people would be embarrassed to carry. That stigma matters as much as the forgetting.

I wanted reminders that fit into a person's day and body: worn like jewellery, glanced at like a watch, or sitting on the table, using vibration, light and colour instead of a screen.

My role: Problem framing, pain points, concept sketches for three directions

A moment that stayed with me
The stigma matters as much as the forgetting.

If a reminder looks like a hospital device, people leave it at home.

What I noticed

Forgetfulness and irregular routines, confusion between medicines, reminders that aren't accessible or portable, and poor engagement.

How it works

Three ways to remind, for three kinds of people. Each uses the same language: vibration to get attention, light to show where, and colour to show which medicine.

Concept 1

Interactive necklace

For people who want a reminder no one else notices.

  1. Wear it like jewellery

Watch dose band

For people who already wear a watch and want it always on them.

  1. Set it once

    Buttons set the hour and minutes, with reset and delete for each alarm.

  2. Feel it, see it

    At dose time it vibrates and a cue light glows, which helps in low light or with hearing loss.

  3. Read the dose

    Colour strips show the type and the quantity, for example three tablets and one syrup.

Concept 3

MediClock+

For people managing many medicines at home.

  1. Organised by day and time

    A week of compartments, split into morning, afternoon, evening and night.

  2. The right box lights up

  3. Made for real hands

    Colour-coded medicines, big easy handles, a detachable mini box for travel, and an eye-drop tool for precise aim.

Women aged 10–55, social service agencies, anyone learning about periods

The gap

Periods are discussed more openly, but tracking apps still feel clinical and stop at dates.

What I did

My first full UX case study: research, personas, journeys, IA and 19 wireframes.

What came out

An app that tracks, cares and connects, with a warm tone.

Why I did this

Periods are talked about more openly than they were a few decades ago. But care hasn't caught up.

Cramps, mood swings, bloating, the constant fear of staining, and the effort of remembering dates still fall on women every month, and many quietly neglect their own health and wellness. 54% of women say remembering their cycle date isn't easy.

The apps I looked at (Flo, Premom, Glow Fertility, Maya, Lunar) were mostly about tracking. I wanted one warm space that also cares for the person: mood, exercise, diet, community, and a way to find agencies that donate sanitary pads.

My role: Solo UX case study: research, personas, empathy maps, journey, information architecture, site map, wireframes and visual direction

A moment that stayed with me
It's that time of the month.

The line my case study opened with. I wanted the app to talk the way women actually talk to each other.

What it does

  1. Tracks and predicts

    Periods and fertility window, and helps you understand your own patterns.

  2. Looks after you

    Verified exercises and meditation, music for your mood, water reminders, and a verified diet plan.

  3. Teaches and connects

    Guides to periods and self-care, a community tab with a secret mode, nearby medical stores, and trusted agencies to donate sanitary pads.

Who it's for

Two personas and their empathy maps kept the design grounded in what people say, do, think and feel about period apps. Their frustrations were clear: too many ads, paid subscriptions, unverified content and cluttered design.

Structure

A user journey showed where people feel low and high. The information architecture and site map organise the app into Calendar & Reminders, Community, Insights, Be You and Shop.

From paper to flow

19 paper wireframes, from splash and onboarding to the questions that set up your cycle, and the home, community and insights screens.

What I learned

Where my UX started

Research, personas, IA and wireframes, all in one project, for the first time.

Tone is a feature

How an app speaks matters as much as what it tracks.

What I'd do now

Test with real users and design for Indian households, privacy and shared phones.

What I did

Built a small design system from Jaipur hand-block print, then iterated through four directions.

What came out

A portfolio that feels like my practice: warm, rooted, clear and a little playful.

Why I did this

A portfolio is the first design a recruiter experiences, long before they read a case study.

My work is about context: designing for real people in real places in India. A generic template would have said the opposite. I wanted the site itself to show how I think: rooted in culture, careful with words, and made for the person on the other side of the screen.

My role: Art direction, information architecture, UX writing, design system and every content decision

How it evolved

  1. Version 1: Clean and editorial

    Clear, but cold. It could have been anyone's portfolio.

  2. Version 2: My practice notebook

    Sticky notes and pink, borrowed from my practice journal. Personal, but too cute for a professional read.

  3. Version 3: Back to my roots

    Inspired by portfolios that carry their maker's culture, I chose Jaipur hand-block print, the craft of the city where my biggest project happened.

  4. Version 4: Rounds of feedback

    Photo, readability, motion, colour per category, and rewriting every project so it reads like a story, not a report.
